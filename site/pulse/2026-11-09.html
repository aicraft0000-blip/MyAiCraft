Three things I picked from the past week. Two change what everyday AI apps can do with your money and your photos. The third is a scam that's doing well.

Claude can now read your bank accounts, if you're in the US

On 5 November Anthropic started a test of Personal Finance in Claude. You connect your bank, card, loan and investment accounts, and then ask Claude questions about your money, like where it went last month. It's only for paid Claude plans in the US so far, and it's rolling out gradually.

What it can and can't do matters more than the feature itself. It can only read: it can't move, pay or invest anything. The connection goes through a service called Plaid, so Claude never sees your bank password. It reads balances, credit limits and up to two years of transactions. Anthropic's own announcement says Claude can make mistakes with the numbers, and suggests asking which transactions are behind any figure and checking anything important with your bank.

If this comes to the UK, the same habit applies as with any app that asks to connect to your bank: check what it can see, check what it can do, and keep the ability to switch it off.

Sources: Anthropic's announcement, Claude help centre.

ChatGPT can now keep photos of you to reuse

OpenAI released Images 2.6 on 4 November. Two parts are new for everyday users. You can save a reference photo under a name and reuse it later without uploading it again. OpenAI's own example is a photo of a dog saved as "Pepper". And ChatGPT can now make short looping animations (GIFs) from a description or a sketch.

The reference photos are the part to think about. Saving a photo of your pet is one thing. Saving photos of yourself, or of your children, means they sit in your ChatGPT account to be reused. It's worth deciding which pictures you're comfortable leaving there, and checking how to delete them.

Sources: OpenAI help centre: Images in ChatGPT, ChatGPT release notes.

A phone scam pretending to be Amazon

Report Fraud, the national fraud reporting service run by City of London Police (it replaced Action Fraud), issued an alert on 30 October. Between 5 and 14 October it received 110 reports of people being phoned by someone claiming to be from Amazon, with losses of about £86,837. The usual pattern is a recorded message about a problem with your account, then a "press one" that puts you through to a fake customer service agent.

This one isn't new or especially high-tech, but cloned voices and AI-written scripts make these calls more convincing every month. If a call about your account asks for money, codes or access to your computer, hang up and contact the company yourself. In the UK you can ring 159 to reach your bank's fraud team safely.

Sources: reported by Britbrief and the Sussex Express. I couldn't find Report Fraud's own page for this alert, so treat the exact figures as reported, not confirmed.
