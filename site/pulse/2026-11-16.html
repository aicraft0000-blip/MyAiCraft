Three things I picked from the past week. One is a deadline for people who talk to ChatGPT. One is about who's listening in a meeting. The third is for anyone running a business with AI answering customers.

ChatGPT's older voices go on 11 December

If you talk to ChatGPT out loud, this one has a date on it. OpenAI is retiring its two older voice options, Standard and Advanced, on 11 December. After that, everyone is moved to the newer "Live" voice. OpenAI told users on 10 November and says you can keep using the old voices until then.

I picked this because of the reaction. A lot of people have written to OpenAI asking to keep Standard voice, some because it suits the way they think and listen. If you rely on voice, for comfort, for accessibility or just habit, try Live now while you can still switch back, so 11 December isn't a surprise.

Sources: OpenAI help centre: Retiring Advanced and Standard Voice, ChatGPT Voice.

Claude can now sit in your video calls

Since 12 November, Claude can join a Google Meet call as a visible participant. It takes notes, answers questions and writes up documents while the call is going on, and saves a transcript and summary (not the audio). It's a test for Claude's Team and Enterprise plans, so it's aimed at workplaces rather than home users, and on Team plans inviting Claude is switched on by default.

The part worth knowing even if you never use it: if Claude, or any note-taking AI, is in a meeting, everything said is being written down. If you're the one adding it, tell people first and give them the chance to say no. If you're on a call and see an AI participant you didn't expect, it's fine to ask what it's keeping and who'll see it.

Sources: Anthropic: Introducing Claude Meetings, Claude help centre: Use Claude Meetings.

If your AI talks to customers, what it says is on you

On 30 October four UK regulators (the Competition and Markets Authority, the Financial Conduct Authority, the Information Commissioner's Office and Ofcom) published a joint statement about AI agents: programs that act on someone's behalf, like answering customers, booking or buying. Their message is simple. Existing law already applies, and an agent acting on its own doesn't reduce the responsibility of the business that put it there. The CMA says an agent that steers, pressures or misleads customers in ways that harm them is likely to be unlawful.

For a small business, that means checking what your chatbot or AI assistant actually tells people, keeping a record of what it said, and making sure a customer can always reach a person when something goes wrong.

Source: Digital Regulation Cooperation Forum: joint statement on the agentic economy. I could only read excerpts of it through search, not the whole document.
