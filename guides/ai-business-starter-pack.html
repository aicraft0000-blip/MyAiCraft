Writing & Communication

01

Client Proposal

Use when you need to write a proposal for a new client without spending two hours on it.
You are writing a business proposal on my behalf. My business: [describe what you do in 2 sentences] The client: [name of client and what they do] What they need: [the problem or project they've described] What I'm proposing to do: [your solution or service] Price: [your fee or pricing structure] Timeline: [when you'd deliver or start] Write a professional proposal with these sections: 1. Opening — acknowledge their situation and what they're trying to achieve 2. Our approach — how I would tackle this specifically 3. What's included — a clear list of deliverables 4. Investment — the price with a brief justification 5. Next step — one clear action for them to take Tone: confident, direct, no jargon. Write as if you know the client reasonably well — warm but professional. Maximum 400 words.
Why it works: Giving the AI both sides of the conversation (your business AND the client's situation) means it can write something specific rather than generic. The section structure prevents waffle.
02

Complaint Response

Use when a customer is unhappy and you need to respond in a way that keeps the relationship intact.
A customer has complained and I need to write a response. What they complained about: [describe the complaint] What actually happened from my side: [your honest account] What I'm prepared to do about it: [refund, redo, discount, apology only — be specific] Write a response that: - Opens by acknowledging their experience (not dismissing it) - Explains what happened briefly and honestly — no deflecting - States clearly what I'm doing to resolve it - Closes in a way that gives them a reason to stay Tone: genuine, calm, not defensive. Under 200 words. No corporate language ("we apologise for any inconvenience" is banned).
Why it works: Banning specific phrases forces the AI away from the canned responses that make customers angrier. The honest account section stops it from making promises you can't keep.
03

Cold Outreach Email

Use when you want to reach out to a potential client or partner and not sound like everyone else.
Write a cold outreach email for me. Who I'm contacting: [name, role, company] Why I'm contacting them specifically: [something specific about them — a post they wrote, a problem their business has, a recent announcement] What I do: [one clear sentence] What I'm asking for: [a call, a reply, a specific question — keep it one thing] Rules: - Subject line must be under 8 words and not use the word "quick" - First sentence must reference something specific about them — not a generic compliment - Body must be under 100 words - One clear ask at the end — not multiple options - No "I hope this finds you well" or "I wanted to reach out"
Why it works: Cold emails fail because they're about the sender. These constraints force the email to lead with the recipient's world before introducing yours.
04

Meeting Summary

Use right after a call or meeting — turn messy notes into a clean summary with actions.
Turn these rough meeting notes into a clean summary. Meeting notes: [paste your notes — even messy bullet points or half-sentences work] Attendees: [names and roles] Date: [date] Format the output as: - 3-sentence overview of what was discussed and decided - Action items: each on its own line, with the owner's name and a deadline if mentioned - Any open questions or things left unresolved - Suggested date for follow-up if relevant Keep the whole thing under 300 words. Plain language only.
Why it works: Messy notes go in, clean output comes out. The structured format means you can paste it straight into an email or project tool without editing.
05

Team Update

Use for weekly or monthly updates to your team, stakeholders, or investors.
Write a team/stakeholder update for me. Period: [week/month and dates] What went well: [bullet points — even rough] What didn't go as planned: [be honest here — AI won't judge] What I'm focused on next: [priorities for the coming period] Any decisions I need input on: [optional] Format: short opening paragraph (2 sentences), then three sections — Wins, Challenges, What's Next. End with one honest sentence about where things stand overall. Tone: direct, no spin. This is for people who need to know what's actually happening, not a PR exercise. Under 250 words.
Why it works: The "no spin" instruction keeps the AI from inflating good news and burying bad — which is what makes these updates actually trusted.

Research & Strategy

06

Competitor Analysis

Use when you want to understand how a competitor is positioned and where they're vulnerable.
Analyse this competitor for me. Competitor: [name and website if you have it] My business: [what you do and who you serve] I want to understand: 1. How they position themselves — what problem do they claim to solve and for whom 2. What they charge and how their pricing is structured 3. Where their offer looks strong 4. Where their offer looks weak or incomplete 5. What a customer choosing them over me is probably prioritising Be direct. I'm not looking for a balanced academic summary — I want to know where the gap is that I can exploit.
Why it works: Asking for the exploitable gap rather than a balanced view forces the AI to give you something actionable rather than a Wikipedia entry.
07

Customer Research Summary

Use when you have reviews or feedback and want to understand what customers are really saying.
Analyse this customer feedback and tell me what it's really saying. Source: [where this came from — reviews, survey, interviews, support tickets] My product/service: [one sentence on what you sell] Feedback: [paste the reviews or feedback — as many as you have] Tell me: 1. The 3 things customers value most — in their own language, not a summary 2. The 3 things that frustrate or disappoint them most 3. What kind of person is buying this — their situation, not just their demographics 4. One thing this feedback suggests I should change or add 5. One thing it suggests I should stop doing or saying Don't summarise what I already gave you. Tell me what's underneath it.
Why it works: "In their own language" and "what's underneath it" pull the AI away from paraphrasing and toward genuine insight.
08

Pricing Sense-Check

Use when you're not sure if your pricing is right and want an honest outside view.
I want an honest assessment of my pricing. What I sell: [describe your product or service clearly] What I currently charge: [your prices] Who I sell to: [describe your customer — size, budget, sophistication] My main competitors and their prices (if known): [list them] Tell me: 1. Whether my pricing looks too low, too high, or roughly right — and why 2. What my pricing signals to a potential customer seeing it for the first time 3. Whether my structure makes sense for this market 4. One thing I should consider changing Be direct. Don't tell me what I want to hear.
Why it works: "Be direct. Don't tell me what I want to hear" genuinely changes AI output — it's less likely to validate whatever you've already done.
09

Market Gap Finder

Use when you're thinking about a new product or service and want to find where the real opportunity is.
Help me find the gap in this market. Market or category: [e.g. "online fitness coaching for women over 40"] Look at this from three angles: 1. Who is being underserved — a customer type the main players aren't focused on 2. What problem exists that nobody is solving well 3. What the current options get wrong — the frustration that keeps coming up For each angle, give me one specific, concrete opportunity — not a vague suggestion. Tell me who I'd be selling to, what I'd be selling, and why the current options aren't enough.
Why it works: Forcing three separate angles prevents one obvious answer. "Specific and concrete" stops the AI from staying vague.
10

SWOT in Plain English

Use when you need to think clearly about a decision, a new direction, or the current state of your business.
Run a plain-English SWOT analysis on this. The situation: [describe the business decision, opportunity, or challenge] Context about my business: [2-3 sentences — what you do, your size, your market] Rules: - No bullet point padding — every point must be a real, specific observation - Maximum 3 points per section - Strengths and Weaknesses should be internal (things I control) - Opportunities and Threats should be external (things in the market) - End with one sentence: given all of this, what's the most honest single thing I should do next?
Why it works: "No bullet point padding" is the single most useful instruction you can give an AI on a SWOT. It forces quality over quantity.

Cash Flow Summary

Use when you want a clear picture of your cash position without staring at a spreadsheet.
Help me understand my cash position. Money coming in this month: [list expected income — invoices, subscriptions, one-off payments] Money going out this month: [list expected expenses — rent, payroll, subscriptions, suppliers] Current bank balance: [approximate figure] Any unusual items: [late payments, large one-off costs, upcoming tax bill] Tell me: 1. My expected end-of-month balance 2. Whether I have a cash gap risk and when it would hit 3. The one expense or receivable I should focus on this week 4. One question I should be asking myself about this picture Plain numbers, plain language. No financial jargon.
Why it works: Most people avoid looking at cash flow because it feels complicated. This produces something you can read in 60 seconds.
12

Invoice Chaser

Use when a client hasn't paid and you need to chase them without being a pushover.
Write an invoice chasing email for me. Invoice details: [invoice number, amount, original due date] How overdue: [number of days] Client relationship: [new client / long-term / difficult / usually pays on time] Have I chased before: [yes — how many times / no — this is the first] Tone I want: [polite reminder / firm / final warning before escalation] Write one email. Include: - A clear reference to the invoice (number and amount in the first line) - The number of days overdue stated plainly - A specific request for payment by a specific date - What happens next if it isn't paid (if this is a firm or final email) No apologising for asking to be paid. No "just" or "only" ("I just wanted to follow up" is banned).
Why it works: "No apologising for asking to be paid" removes the hedging language that makes invoice chasers easy to ignore.
13

Contract Red-Flag Scan

Use when you've received a contract and want to know what to watch out for before signing.
Review this contract and tell me what to watch out for. Contract type: [employment / freelance / supplier / client / NDA / lease] My position: [am I the client, supplier, employee, employer?] Contract text: [paste the contract or the sections you're concerned about] I want: 1. The top 3 clauses I should push back on or negotiate — and why 2. Anything that's missing that should be in a contract like this 3. Any language that's unusually one-sided in the other party's favour 4. A plain-English summary of what I'm actually agreeing to Note: this is not legal advice. Flag anything serious and I'll take it to a solicitor.
Why it works: The disclaimer keeps the AI from being overly cautious. The "missing clauses" question catches things that aren't there — which prompts often miss.
14

SOP From a Voice Note

Use when you need to document a process for a new hire, VA, or just to stop doing it from memory.
Turn this voice note transcript into a Standard Operating Procedure. What the process is: [name the process — e.g. "onboarding a new client"] Who will follow this SOP: [employee / VA / contractor / yourself] Voice note transcript: [paste your transcript — messy is fine, even "um" and tangents] Format it as: - Process name and purpose (1 sentence) - What you need before you start (tools, access, information) - Numbered steps — clear and specific enough that someone new could follow them - What done looks like - Common mistakes or things to watch out for Plain language. If I said something ambiguous, make a reasonable assumption and note it in [brackets] so I can check it.
Why it works: Speaking for 3 minutes and passing the transcript to AI produces a usable first draft in seconds. Most SOPs fail because writing them from scratch takes too long.
15

Job Description Writer

Use when you need to hire someone and want a job description that attracts the right people.
Write a job description for me. Role title: [job title] What this person will actually do day-to-day: [be specific — list the main tasks] What I need them to have: [non-negotiable skills or experience] What would be nice but isn't essential: [desirable extras] Working arrangement: [full-time / part-time / freelance / remote / hybrid] Salary or rate: [the range, or "competitive"] What kind of company this is: [2 sentences — size, what you do, culture in one word] Write it as: - Short intro about the role and why it exists (2 sentences) - What you'll be doing (bullet list, max 6 points) - What we're looking for (Essential and Desirable split) - What we offer - How to apply Do not use: rockstar, ninja, passionate, fast-paced environment, must thrive under pressure. Honest and direct only.
Why it works: The banned words list removes the clichés that signal a bad employer to good candidates. Asking what the person will "actually do day-to-day" stops the description becoming a wish list.
