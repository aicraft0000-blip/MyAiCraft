The One-Page Business Plan Template

Plan Your Business on a Single Page — With Examples and Instructions

Business plans don't need to be 40 pages long. Most of the useful information fits on one page. The rest is padding that nobody reads — not even investors. This guide gives you a one-page template, clear instructions for each section, two completed examples, and the most common mistakes to avoid. Print the template. Fill it in. You'll have more clarity about your business in 30 minutes than most people get in 30 days.

How to Fill In Each Section

Business Name and What You Sell

Your "what you sell" sentence should follow this formula:

I help [specific person] [achieve specific result] through [your method].

Bad example: "I offer marketing services to businesses." Good example: "I help independent coffee shops get more foot traffic through Google Business profile management and local SEO." The more specific, the better. Specificity makes marketing easier, pricing clearer, and sales conversations shorter.

Who You Sell To

Describe your ideal customer with enough detail that you could find them in a room. Include: their industry, size, location (if relevant), and the situation that makes them need your product. Bad example: "Small businesses." Good example: "Solo fitness coaches with 500–5,000 Instagram followers who sell online training programmes and struggle with getting enquiries from their content."

The Problem and Your Solution

Every business solves a problem. If you can't name the problem, you'll struggle to sell the solution. The problem should be something your customer would say out loud. Not business jargon — their words. Example: "I'm posting on social media every day but nobody's buying." Your solution should explain how you fix it differently from what they've already tried.

Revenue Model

List every way you'll make money. Be specific about pricing. Common models: Service fees (charging per project or per hour), Product sales (physical or digital at a set price), Subscriptions/retainers (monthly recurring revenue), Commissions/affiliate income (earning a percentage on referrals). Your monthly revenue target should be grounded in reality: "If I sell X units at £Y each, that's £Z per month."

Costs

Separate one-time startup costs from monthly running costs. Startup costs might include: website domain and hosting (£100/year), equipment (£0–£2,000), initial inventory, legal setup (£50–£500), branding and logo (£0–£500). Monthly costs might include: software subscriptions, marketing spend, insurance, accounting. Your break-even point is when monthly revenue equals monthly costs — the single most important financial milestone for any new business.

Marketing — How Customers Find You

Pick ONE primary channel. Not five. One. If your customers are on LinkedIn, focus on LinkedIn. If they search Google for your service, focus on SEO. You can add channels later. Splitting your effort across five platforms from day one means you'll do none of them well.

Competitive Advantage

You need to know who else solves this problem and why someone would pick you. Common advantages: Speed (you deliver faster), Specialisation (you only serve their industry), Price (you're more affordable — careful, this is a race to the bottom), Experience (you've been in their shoes), Service (you provide better communication and support). Pick one or two and build your marketing around them.

90-Day Milestones

Make these measurable. "Grow the business" is not a milestone. "Sign 3 paying clients at £400/month each" is. Good month 1 milestones: launch website, set up payment processing, contact first 20 potential customers, make first sale. Good month 2 milestones: get first repeat customer or referral, reach £500 in revenue. Good month 3 milestones: hit £1,000 in monthly revenue, get first testimonial, refine pricing based on what you've learned.

5 Common Mistakes to Avoid

Mistake 1: Writing for investors instead of yourself

If you're a solo operator or small business, this plan is for YOU. It's your roadmap. Write it in language you understand, with numbers that reflect your reality. Skip the corporate speak.

Mistake 2: Being vague about your customer

"Everyone" is not a target market. The more specific your customer description, the easier every other section becomes. When you know exactly who you serve, you know where to find them, what to say, and how to price.

Mistake 3: Ignoring costs (or underestimating them)

New business owners consistently underestimate costs by 30–50%. Include everything: software, transaction fees, taxes, your own time, returns, and a buffer for unexpected expenses. If you think it'll cost £500/month, plan for £700.

Mistake 4: Picking five marketing channels

You have limited time. One channel done well beats five channels done poorly. Pick the platform where your customers already spend time. Master it. Add a second channel only after the first one is producing results.

Mistake 5: Setting milestones without deadlines

"Get more clients" is a wish. "Sign 3 clients by March 15th" is a milestone. Every goal on your plan needs a number, a deadline, and a way to measure whether you've hit it. Review your milestones at the end of each month and adjust based on reality, not optimism.
